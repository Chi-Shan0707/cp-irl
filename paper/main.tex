\begin{abstract}
Inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) is inverse optimization with a sequential
forward problem: demonstrations reveal an unknown reward, and the recovered
reward is subsequently optimized to prescribe a policy. This perspective exposes
the same weakness identified by Conformal Inverse Optimization (CIO) for one-shot
decisions: collapsing heterogeneous objectives to one point estimate can yield a
bad downstream decision. We introduce \textbf{\method{}}, which transfers CIO's
calibrate-then-robustify principle to Markov decision processes. From a population
of demonstrators, \method{} conformally calibrates a set of reward parameters
consistent with a new demonstrator and optimizes a policy against the resulting
reward ambiguity. The sequential setting requires new machinery. We reduce each
calibration score to a reward-dimensional convex program using the Bellman
resolvent, and prove that a fixed transition kernel makes the robust MDP convex
for any compact reward ambiguity set; a two-state counterexample shows that this
property can fail when ambiguity also enters the dynamics. We establish
finite-sample coverage, regret bounds, and a separation example in which
point-estimate IRL has unbounded regret while \method{}'s regret vanishes.
Experiments on random MDPs, gridworld, and Objectworld identify the method's
scope: robustification reduces true-reward regret under systematic estimation
bias, but incurs an insurance cost when the point estimate is merely noisy and
approximately unbiased.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) is usually introduced as a distinct
learning problem: infer a reward from behavior, then optimize that reward to
prescribe a policy. Viewed end to end, however, IRL is a sequential instance of
\emph{inverse optimization}. The observed decision is a policy or trajectory,
the latent objective is a reward function, and the forward problem is an MDP.
This connection is more than terminology. It suggests that uncertainty about
the recovered objective should be propagated into the downstream decision,
rather than discarded after producing a point estimate.

Conformal Inverse Optimization (CIO) formalizes exactly this idea for one-shot
problems~\cite{lin2024conformal}. CIO replaces a single recovered objective with
a conformally calibrated uncertainty set and optimizes robustly over that set.
Its guarantee is finite-sample and distribution-free under exchangeability,
and its separation example shows why the change matters: point-estimate inverse
optimization can retain unbounded downstream regret even with unlimited data.
The natural question motivating this paper is therefore:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Can CIO's calibrate-then-robustify principle be transferred from one-shot
inverse optimization to inverse reinforcement learning without losing coverage
or tractability?}
\end{quote}

We answer this question with \method{}. A population of demonstrators supplies
the exchangeable calibration units; each observed policy defines a feasible
reward set through Bellman optimality; split conformal prediction calibrates a
spherical cap around any chosen IRL point estimator; and a robust MDP converts
that calibrated reward uncertainty into a deployed policy. Thus, the conceptual
pipeline is inherited from CIO, while the technical work lies in adapting and
optimizing it for sequential decisions.

Making the CIO template work for IRL is not a mechanical transplant. Two
structural obstacles are specific to the sequential setting:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The nonconformity score is no longer an LP-duality fact.} CIO's
  per-point calibration score is computable in closed form because the forward
  problem is a linear program and reward feasibility is characterized by LP dual
  feasibility. In an MDP, ``this policy is optimal for this reward'' is a
  Bellman-optimality condition, not an LP-duality condition, and computing it
  naively requires jointly optimizing over the reward parameter \emph{and} the
  policy's value function -- a variable count that grows with the state space.
\item \textbf{The robust decision problem is a minimax game over policies, not
  points.} CIO's robust forward problem is a single robust linear program. The
  MDP analogue is a robust MDP: $\max_\pi \min_\theta$ over an ambiguity set.
  General robust MDPs with ambiguity that couples across states are
  NP-hard~\cite{wiesemann2013robust}; most tractable results in the literature
  rely on \emph{rectangularity} assumptions that restrict how the adversary's
  choice can vary across states.
\end{enumerate}

We resolve both obstacles and develop the MDP counterpart of CIO's principal
guarantees. The contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Transfer: conformal inverse optimization for IRL.}
  We formulate one demonstrator as one exchangeable inverse-optimization
  instance, calibrate a reward uncertainty set through Bellman-optimal reward
  feasibility, and establish finite-sample coverage together with perceived-
  and true-regret bounds (\S\ref{sec:setting}--\S\ref{sec:theory}).
\item \textbf{Optimization: sequential structure without state-sized calibration}
  (\S\ref{sec:method-calibration}). For a fixed demonstrator policy, the induced
  value function is \emph{linear} in the reward parameter via the Bellman
  resolvent $(I - \gamma P_\pi)^{-1}$; substituting this collapses the
  per-demonstrator calibration score from a joint optimization over $(\theta, V)$
  -- size $d + |S|$ -- to an optimization over $\theta$ alone -- size $d$ -- with
  no relaxation, by the policy improvement theorem. We further give a
  Sherman--Morrison--Woodbury update that recomputes this resolvent in
  $O(k^2|S| + k^3)$ rather than $O(|S|^3)$ when two demonstrators' policies
  differ at only $k$ states, motivated by and tailored to the
  population-of-demonstrators setting.
\item \textbf{Tractability: a boundary between reward and dynamics ambiguity}
  (\S\ref{sec:method-robust}). We show the robust MDP is a convex program in
  occupancy-measure space whenever ambiguity is confined to the reward parameter
  \emph{for any compact ambiguity set}, via a Bellman-recursion-free argument,
  and -- new relative to the closest existing sufficiency-side result
  (Gadot et al.~\cite{gadot2024solving}, who solve the convex $L_p$-ball case via
  H\"older duality and policy gradient) -- we prove this is \emph{tight}: an
  explicit two-state, two-action MDP with a one-dimensional, convex, non-product
  ambiguity set coupling reward and transition probability breaks concavity,
  with a hand-verifiable rational-valued Jensen violation.
\item \textbf{Scope: robustness helps under bias, not noise alone.}
  Our sequential analogue of CIO's separation example proves that classical
  point-estimate IRL can have diverging regret while the calibrated policy's
  regret vanishes. Across random MDPs, a
  region-based gridworld, and Objectworld, we find that calibrated robust
  hedging reduces true-reward regret specifically when the point estimate is
  \emph{systematically} biased (as our degenerate construction manufactures),
  and \emph{increases} regret when the estimate is merely noisy but unbiased --
  a precise, falsifiable boundary condition for the method's benefit that we
  report as a finding, not a limitation to be hidden.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Inverse optimization and its calibration.} Inverse optimization
recovers parameters of an optimization problem from observed decisions
(Ahuja \& Orlin~\cite{ahuja2001inverse}; Chan et al.~\cite{chan2018inverse}).
Classical formulations return a point estimate; more recent work constructs
data-driven uncertainty sets around it (Chenreddy, Bandi \&
Delage~\cite{chenreddy2022datadriven}; Sun, Liu \& Li~\cite{sun2023predictthencalibrate}),
but -- as Lin, Delage \& Chan~\cite{lin2024conformal} show -- none of these give
the specific guarantee we need: that the \emph{next} observed decision's true
parameter is covered with a stated confidence, distribution-free. Conformal
Inverse Optimization is the first to close this gap using split conformal
prediction~\cite{vovk2005algorithmic}, and is the paper this work directly
extends from one-shot decisions to MDPs.

\textbf{Feasible reward sets in IRL.} A separate line of work characterizes the
\emph{set} of rewards consistent with observed IRL data
(Poiani et al.~\cite{poiani2024suboptimal}; Lazzati \& Metelli~\cite{lazzati2025robustness};
Skalse et al.~\cite{skalse2022invariance}) and proves PAC-style bounds on
recovering it, using it directly as a robust-MDP ambiguity set. This line
shares our target object -- a feasible reward set characterized by
Bellman-optimality constraints -- but its guarantees are PAC bounds requiring a
generative model of demonstrator sub-optimality; ours require only
exchangeability of demonstrators, no assumption on the \emph{process} by which
any individual demonstrator deviates from optimality. The transfer question
this line raises -- whether a feasible reward set identified on one MDP
transfers to another (Schlaginhaufen \& Kamgarpour~\cite{schlaginhaufen2024towards})
-- is a genuinely open problem for our setting that we do not attempt to close
here (see \S\ref{sec:discussion}).

\textbf{Robust MDPs and the rectangularity boundary.} Robust MDPs with
ambiguity coupling across states are NP-hard in general
(Wiesemann, Kuhn \& Rustem~\cite{wiesemann2013robust}); tractability is
classically recovered via \emph{s-} or \emph{sa-rectangularity}
(Iyengar~\cite{iyengar2005robust}; Nilim \& El Ghaoui~\cite{nilim2005robust}),
which restricts the adversary to choose independently per state. Recent work
extends tractable, \emph{non}-rectangular ambiguity specifically for
reward-only uncertainty with a fixed transition kernel: Gadot et
al.~\cite{gadot2024solving} solve the convex $L_p$-ball case exactly via
H\"older duality and a policy-gradient method with proven convergence; Xu et
al.'s Group-Robust MDPs~\cite{xu2026learning} handle \emph{feature-wise}
(d-rectangular) ambiguity that couples both reward \emph{and} transition, at
the cost of restricting to that rectangular structure. Our sufficiency result
(\S\ref{sec:method-robust}) covers the same fixed-kernel, reward-only regime as
Gadot et al. but for an \emph{arbitrary} compact ambiguity set (not only
$L_p$-balls), via a direct occupancy-measure convex program rather than a
policy-gradient method; our necessity result -- an explicit demonstration that
convexity is lost the moment ambiguity couples into the kernel, even minimally
-- has no counterpart in either paper.

\textbf{Conformal prediction in RL-adjacent settings.} Conformal prediction has
been used for offline-RL pessimism from Q-value or state-action-coverage
uncertainty (UARM, Pan et al.~\cite{pan2026uncertaintyaware}), for calibrated
intervals in infinite-horizon policy evaluation, and for modeling \emph{other
agents' actions} as conformal prediction sets in multi-agent RL (CAMMARL,
Gupta \& Kahou~\cite{gupta2023cammarl}) -- the last of these is explicitly
motivated by an analogy to IRL but never calibrates a reward set. None combine
conformal calibration with a reward vector recovered from a population of IRL
demonstrators and a robust-MDP regret guarantee, which is the gap this paper
closes.

\textbf{Preference learning and RLHF.} Preference-based and RLHF-style learning
fits a reward (or policy) from labeled comparisons or demonstrations, typically
from individual expert trajectories (Christiano et al.~\cite{christiano2017deep};
Rafailov et al.~\cite{rafailov2023direct}) or crowdsourced preference
populations. Our per-demonstrator exchangeable-unit design (\S\ref{sec:setting})
is structurally suited to recast ``one annotator, one calibration point'' for
heterogeneous-preference RLHF populations, giving a coverage guarantee of the
form ``the deployed policy is provably good for at least a $\gamma$-fraction of
a population whose individual reward functions are never directly observed'' --
a guarantee we are not aware of in the RLHF calibration literature -- though we
do not run an RLHF-scale experiment here (see \S\ref{sec:discussion}).

\section{Setting}
\label{sec:setting}

We now make the CIO--IRL correspondence precise. We adopt the
population-of-demonstrators setting used throughout this work
(formalized as Assumption IRL-1 in our coverage proof): a shared tabular MDP
$M = (S, A, P, \beta, \phi)$ with known transition kernel $P$, discount $\beta$,
and feature map $\phi: S \times A \to \mathbb{R}^d$ inducing reward
$r_\theta(s,a) = \phi(s,a)^\top\theta$. A population of demonstrators draws
perceived rewards $\hat\theta_1, \dots, \hat\theta_N \sim P_\theta$ i.i.d.\ and
each acts optimally in $M$ under their own $\hat\theta_k$, producing an observed
policy $\pi_k = \pi^*(\hat\theta_k)$. This is the direct MDP analogue of CIO's
setting, in which each demonstrator plays the role of one (decision, context)
pair -- and it is the setting under which CIO's Theorem 2's exchangeability
argument transplants essentially verbatim (\S\ref{sec:theory}). We commit to
this ``unit A'' design over two weaker alternatives -- per-task exchangeability
with a single demonstrator (still exchangeable, treated as an easy corollary)
and per-trajectory exchangeability within one demonstrator (broken by
within-trajectory Markov dependence, and used only as an ablation) -- and are
explicit throughout about what breaks under each relaxation.

The learning task, mirroring CIO's pipeline, has three steps: (i) split the
population into training and calibration sets; (ii) fit a point estimate
$\bar\theta$ on the training set with any IRL method (we use and compare a
sub-optimality-loss linear program, MaxEnt IRL, and Bayesian IRL by MCMC --
\S\ref{sec:experiments}); (iii) calibrate a spherical-cap uncertainty set
$C(\bar\theta, \alpha) = \{\theta : \|\theta\|_2 = 1,\ \theta^\top\bar\theta \geq
\cos\alpha\}$ on the calibration set and solve the robust MDP
$\max_\pi \min_{\theta \in C(\bar\theta,\alpha)} \theta^\top \mu(\pi)$ in
occupancy-measure space, where $\mu(\pi)$ is $\pi$'s occupancy measure.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Tractable calibration via the Bellman resolvent}
\label{sec:method-calibration}

CIO's calibration score for a validation point $k$ is
$c_k = \max_\theta \{\theta^\top\bar\theta : \theta \in \Theta^{\mathrm{OPT}}(\hat x_k, u_k), \|\theta\|_2 \leq 1\}$,
where $\Theta^{\mathrm{OPT}}$ is characterized by LP dual feasibility --
tractable because the forward problem is a linear program. In an MDP, the
analogous feasible set for a policy $\pi$ is
\[
\hat\Theta(\pi) = \{\theta : \|\theta\|_2 \leq 1,\ Q^\pi_\theta(s,a) \leq Q^\pi_\theta(s, \pi(s))\ \forall s, \forall a\},
\]
the exact set of reward vectors for which $\pi$ is Bellman-optimal, by the
policy improvement theorem. Computing this naively requires the joint
optimization $\max_{\theta,V} \theta^\top\bar\theta$ subject to the
Bellman-optimality inequalities written in terms of both $\theta$ and the a
priori unknown value function $V$ -- a variable count of $d + |S|$.

The key fact we use: for a \textbf{fixed} policy $\pi$, the policy's own induced
value function $V^\pi_\theta$ is \emph{linear} in $\theta$, via the Bellman
resolvent:
\[
\begin{aligned}
V^\pi_\theta
  &= (I - \beta P_\pi)^{-1} \Phi_\pi \theta =: W_\pi \theta, \\
Q^\pi_\theta(s,a)
  &= M_\pi(s,a)^\top\theta, \\
M_\pi(s,a)
  &:= \phi(s,a) + \beta P(s,a,\cdot)^\top W_\pi.
\end{aligned}
\]
Substituting collapses the calibration score to
$c_k = \max_\theta \{\theta^\top\bar\theta : \|\theta\|_2 \leq 1,\ M_\pi(s,a)^\top\theta \leq M_\pi(s,\pi(s))^\top\theta\ \forall s,a\}$
-- an optimization over $\theta$ alone (size $d$), with $M_\pi$ precomputed once
per distinct policy via a single resolvent solve. This is exact, not a
relaxation: the reduction is a direct consequence of the policy improvement
theorem, verified against a slow joint-$(\theta,V)$ reference implementation to
solver precision across randomized instances, and against independent value
iteration. The amortized speedup over the joint formulation grows with $|S|$
(2--5.5$\times$ observed at $|S|$ in the low hundreds). When a new
demonstrator's policy differs from an already-resolved one at only $k$ states,
the resolvent updates via a Sherman--Morrison--Woodbury identity in
$O(k^2|S|+k^3)$ rather than a fresh $O(|S|^3)$ solve.

\subsection{A tight tractability boundary for the robust MDP}
\label{sec:method-robust}

The robust decision problem, $\max_\pi \min_{\theta \in C} \theta^\top\mu(\pi)$,
is a minimax game over policies. We work in occupancy-measure space: $M(P)$
denotes the (fixed, since $P$ is known) polytope of achievable occupancy
measures, and the robust objective is
$\max_{d \in M(P)} \min_{\theta \in C} \theta^\top\Phi^\top d$.

\textbf{Sufficiency.} For \emph{any} nonempty compact ambiguity set
$C \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ -- not only the spherical cap --
$g_C(x) := \min_{\theta \in C} \theta^\top x$ is a pointwise infimum of affine
functions of $x$, hence concave, regardless of $C$'s shape; composing with the
linear map $d \mapsto \Phi^\top d$ preserves concavity; and $M(P)$ is a fixed
compact polytope. The robust MDP is therefore a \textbf{convex program}, solved
without any Bellman recursion or rectangularity assumption -- the ambiguity
never touches the feasible set $M(P)$, only the objective.

\textbf{Failure beyond reward-only ambiguity.} The sufficiency argument depends
critically on keeping the transition kernel fixed. We exhibit a 2-state,
2-action MDP with a convex, one-dimensional, \emph{non-product} ambiguity set
coupling reward $\theta(t) = 1+t$ and transition probability $q(t) = 1-t$ for
$t \in [0,1]$. The induced worst-case value, as a function of the agent's
(scalar, single-dimensional) policy parameter $p \in [0,1]$, is
$W(p) = p(1+t)/(1-\beta p q(t))$ minimized over $t$; a closed-form derivation
gives an explicit, hand-verifiable, rational-valued Jensen violation:
$W(5/18) = 10/27 \approx 0.370 < \frac{1}{2}[W(0)+W(5/9)] \approx 0.556$,
independently reproduced via numerical optimization rather than only the
closed-form derivation. The coupling required to break convexity is minimal --
a single scalar $t$ linking reward and transition ambiguity along a line
segment, far short of a fully unconstrained non-rectangular adversary.

\section{Theory}
\label{sec:theory}

We prove five further results, each the direct MDP/IRL analogue of a
corresponding CIO theorem, and each independently spot-checked computationally
against the implemented solvers rather than trusted as derivation alone.

\textbf{Coverage.} For the calibrated angle $\alpha_\gamma$ from Theorem 1's
construction, $\mathbb{P}(\hat\Theta_{\mathrm{new}} \cap C(\bar\theta, \alpha_\gamma) \neq \emptyset) \geq \gamma$,
with the standard split-conformal finite-sample concentration bound
$O(\sqrt{\log N_{\mathrm{val}} / N_{\mathrm{val}}})$ on how close the realized
coverage is to $\gamma$. The proof is a line-for-line transplant of CIO's own
exchangeability argument. A marginal-coverage simulation against the actual
calibration code (150 trials per $(\gamma, N_{\mathrm{val}})$ cell) confirms
coverage $\geq \gamma$ holds cleanly at every tested combination.

\textbf{Regret bounds.} We derive the occupancy-measure Lipschitz constant
governing how much the value function can change under a bounded reward
perturbation, $\nu(d) = \|\Phi^\top d\|_2/(1-\beta) \leq \|\phi\|_{2,\infty}/(1-\beta)$
(the maximum per-state-action feature norm, verified against 200 randomized
MDPs with zero violations), and use it to prove the direct analogue of CIO's
Theorem 3. We are explicit that this is a general worst-case bound, not a tight
one: cross-checked against the exact closed-form regret derived for our
degenerate construction, the general bound's looseness grows with the
construction's scaling parameter, from roughly 8--15$\times$ at the low end of
the range tested to roughly 390--430$\times$ at the high end -- a materially
worse asymptotic gap than CIO's own Example 1 exhibits for its own general bound
(roughly 2.3--4$\times$).

\textbf{Separation.} We construct the direct sequential-decision analogue of
CIO's degenerate Example 1: a single-state, two-action MDP whose reward
features are the \emph{negation} of CIO's original cost features. We prove the
point-estimate IRL solution converges to a degenerate direction
$\theta_u = (1,u)/\sqrt{1+u^2}$, not the true $\theta^*$, and that this makes
classical IRL's true-reward regret grow unboundedly as the scaling parameter
$u \to \infty$, while the calibrated robust policy's regret is bounded and
shrinks to zero. This reduced two-action MDP's robust optimum is a genuine
\emph{mixture}, with exact closed form $p^*(u) = u^2/(1+u^2)$, independent of
the calibration angle -- derived via Sion's minimax theorem and independently
re-verified against the actual implemented solver to $10^{-6}$ precision.
Figure~\ref{fig:separation} shows the live 10-seed computational confirmation.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figures/fig1_separation.pdf}
  \caption{Separation result (\S\ref{sec:theory}): true-reward regret vs.\ the
  scaling parameter $u$, log scale, 10 seeds, shaded regions show $\pm 1$ std.
  Classic IRL's regret (orange) grows monotonically and without bound; \method{}'s

\textbf{Efficiency.} We confirm, empirically, that the calibrated set is not
vacuously wide: with a \emph{unified} noise parameter controlling all sources of
demonstrator population variation simultaneously, the calibrated angle shrinks
monotonically from $27.7^\circ$ to effectively $0^\circ$ as demonstrator-population
noise vanishes.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

Our experiments answer three questions: \emph{(Q1)} does calibration attain its
target coverage without becoming vacuous; \emph{(Q2)} when does robustification
improve downstream regret; and \emph{(Q3)} are the conclusions tied to one IRL
estimator or environment? We evaluate three point estimators -- a
sub-optimality-loss linear program,
MaxEnt IRL, and Bayesian IRL (Metropolis--Hastings posterior mean under a
Boltzmann-rational demonstrator likelihood) -- each combined with three
decision rules -- the plain point estimate, our calibrated robust policy, and a
``concentration baseline'' robust policy whose uncertainty-set radius comes
from a classical Dvoretzky--Kiefer--Wolfowitz concentration bound rather than
the exact split-conformal quantile -- across three environments: uncorrelated
random tabular MDPs, a region-based gridworld with spatially contiguous
(Voronoi) reward features, and Objectworld, the standard IRL benchmark with
smooth, continuous distance-to-object features. All experiments run on tabular
MDPs at a scale ($|S| \leq 36$, $d \leq 5$) chosen to fit within a laptop-class
compute budget (20 CPU cores, no GPU dependency, no commercial solver).

\textbf{Q1: Coverage and efficiency.} Realized coverage
tracks the target $\gamma$ across every environment and estimator tested
(e.g.\ $\sim$0.88--0.89 at $\gamma=0.8$ on the gridworld), and the calibrated
angle responds monotonically and sensibly to $\gamma$, to demonstrator noise,
and to the train/calibration split ratio. The DKW concentration baseline is
consistently and measurably looser than exact conformal calibration at matched
nominal confidence, across every estimator and every environment tested.

\textbf{Q2: The benefit depends on systematic bias.} On our degenerate
construction (\S\ref{sec:theory}), robust hedging dramatically reduces
true-reward regret, because the point estimate there is \emph{systematically}
miscalibrated by construction. On all three generic environments tested --
random MDPs, the gridworld, and Objectworld -- under realistic
demonstrator-population noise, robust hedging \emph{increases} true-reward
regret relative to the plain point estimate, consistently across all three
point estimators, holding across a full sweep of the coverage target $\gamma$
and of the train/calibration split ratio. We verified this is not an
implementation defect three independent ways: the robust solver's reported
worst-case value matches an independent second-order-cone-program oracle to
solver precision; the robust decision's worst-case value is confirmably
\emph{better} than the plain estimate's worst-case value over the same
calibrated set; and we directly measured that these environments' optimal
policies are sensitive to reward direction -- a $14^\circ$ reward perturbation
already changes the optimal action at roughly 10--13\% of states across the
three environments tested (9.6\% Objectworld, 9.9\% gridworld, 13.1\% random
MDPs -- a modest, not dramatic, margin). Critically, Objectworld's lower policy-sensitivity
(measured directly, and lower than both the gridworld and random MDPs) did
\textbf{not} flip the result: robust hedging still costs true-reward regret
there, ruling out landscape smoothness as the deciding factor and leaving
systematic bias in the point estimate as the consistent, mechanistically
understood explanation across all three environments.

\textbf{Q3: The conclusion persists across estimators and environments.}
The gridworld comparison combines all three point estimators with the plain,
conformal-robust, and DKW-robust decision rules. MaxEnt and Bayesian IRL recover
an accurate point direction, whereas the LP estimator has nontrivial error; in
all three cases, conformal robustification is less conservative than DKW but
incurs higher true-reward regret than the corresponding point policy. The same
direction holds in Objectworld. These comparisons separate two claims that are
often conflated: conformal calibration can produce a tighter valid uncertainty
set than a generic concentration bound, yet optimizing for its worst case need
not improve average performance under the true reward.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{When does \method{} help, precisely?} Our theory proves the method
dominates classical point-estimate IRL when the point estimate is
\emph{systematically} biased. Our experiments show that on generic environments
with unbiased-but-noisy point estimates, it does not, and in fact costs regret
in exchange for a worst-case guarantee that is real (independently verified)
but not free. This is a precise, falsifiable scope condition, not a hedge: the
method should be deployed when there is reason to believe point estimation is
subject to a genuine structural bias rather than applied by default whenever
demonstration data is merely noisy. Characterizing which real-world settings
exhibit this kind of systematic bias, and whether it can be diagnosed from data
without knowing the ground truth, is an open problem this paper does not
resolve.

\textbf{What is not yet established.} The general-case regret bound is provably
loose -- up to several hundred-fold on the one instance where we can compare it
against an exact result -- and should be read as a worst-case guarantee, not a
predictor of typical-case performance. A source-to-target probe further shows
that a reward set calibrated on one MDP does \emph{not} safely transfer to a
related MDP with different dynamics: at target coverage $0.8$, changing the
gridworld slip probability from $0.10$ to $0.15$ lowers mean target coverage to
$0.567$ over eight seeds. The probe establishes the failure of naive transfer,
not its remedy. Developing a weighted-conformal correction that combines
covariate-shift weighting with the principal-angle transferability conditions
of Schlaginhaufen \& Kamgarpour~\cite{schlaginhaufen2024towards} remains future
work. We also tested a preference-population/RLHF-style
relabeling of our per-demonstrator design: a ``response selection'' MDP in which
the population is reinterpreted as RLHF annotators rather than task
demonstrators, using the same calibration and robust-MDP code unchanged. The
coverage guarantee transfers exactly as claimed -- realized coverage exceeds the
target $\gamma$ at every level tested (0.947--0.994 across
$\gamma \in \{0.5,0.7,0.8,0.9\}$, 8 seeds) -- confirming the framework
generalizes by relabeling alone, at the cost of the same limitations documented
elsewhere (degenerate low-dimensional calibration geometry in this simple
construction; the same systematic-bias-conditional benefit, not a special
exemption for the RLHF framing). All experiments are at tabular scale by
explicit design choice; we make no claim about function-approximation or
deep-RL/language-model settings.

\section{Conclusion}

IRL is inverse optimization with an MDP as its forward problem. Starting from
this connection, we transfer CIO's calibrate-then-robustify principle to reward
learning and supply the sequential machinery the transfer requires: a
resolvent-based calibration score, a convex robust-MDP formulation for compact
reward ambiguity, and coverage, regret, and separation guarantees. The same
connection also clarifies the method's limits. Calibration certifies uncertainty;
it does not make worst-case optimization free. In our experiments, the resulting
policy improves true-reward regret when point estimation is systematically biased,
but pays an insurance cost when estimation is merely noisy. This distinction is
the practical conclusion of \method{} and the main condition governing its use.
