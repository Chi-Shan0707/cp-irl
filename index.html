Abstract

Inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) is inverse optimization with a sequential forward problem: demonstrations reveal an unknown reward, and the recovered reward is subsequently optimized to prescribe a policy. This perspective exposes the same weakness identified by Conformal Inverse Optimization (CIO) for one-shot decisions: collapsing heterogeneous objectives to one point estimate can yield a bad downstream decision. We introduce CP‑IRL, which transfers CIO's calibrate-then-robustify principle to Markov decision processes. From a population of demonstrators, CP‑IRL conformally calibrates a set of reward parameters consistent with a new demonstrator and optimizes a policy against the resulting reward ambiguity. The sequential setting requires new machinery: we reduce each calibration score to a reward-dimensional convex program using the Bellman resolvent, and prove that a fixed transition kernel makes the robust MDP convex for any compact reward ambiguity set — a two-state counterexample shows this property can fail once ambiguity also enters the dynamics. We establish finite-sample coverage, regret bounds, and a separation example in which point-estimate IRL has unbounded regret while CP‑IRL's regret vanishes. Experiments on random MDPs, gridworld, and Objectworld identify the method's scope: robustification reduces true-reward regret under systematic estimation bias, but incurs an insurance cost when the point estimate is merely noisy and approximately unbiased.

Three things worth remembering

1. A matrix identity turns an intractable joint problem into a tractable one

Calibrating each demonstrator naively requires jointly solving over the reward parameter (dimension d) and the value function (dimension |S|, often in the thousands). Because the value function is an exact linear function of the reward under a fixed policy (the Bellman resolvent), the problem collapses to dimension d — no approximation involved.

2. The convexity boundary is found and pinned down with a minimal counterexample

Robust MDPs stay convex for any reward ambiguity set as long as transition dynamics are known exactly. A two-state, two-action example — verified by hand with rational arithmetic — shows convexity breaks the moment ambiguity touches the transition kernel, even minimally.

3. Robust hedging helps under bias, hurts under pure noise — reported honestly

Across random MDPs, gridworld, and Objectworld, robustification's benefit depends on the nature of point-estimate error, not just its size: large gains under systematic bias, net losses under unbiased noise. Both regimes are reported as core results.
